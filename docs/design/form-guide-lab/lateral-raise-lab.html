Lateral Raise Lab

The dumbbell lateral raise, built on a new engine. A shaped 3D body lifts your 7 kg dumbbells. You can turn it to any angle or zoom into the shoulder. Three parts of the deltoid and the upper trap swell and shorten as they work. The load comes from physics, and the common mistake plays beside the right rep.

    How it's built

    Every exercise shares one skeleton and one body. An exercise is only a short motion description. The engine computes everything else from it, in this order:

    1. Motion description

      Joint angles over time, the movement plane, the grip, the tempo and the cues. It also describes the common mistake, so the mistake is the same exercise done wrong, not a second drawing.

    2. Shared skeleton

      Segment lengths are standard fractions of body height (1.78 m here). A second joint moves the shoulder blade: above 30° it rotates about 1° for every 2° the arm rises, as a real shoulder does.

    3. Body and clothes

      Smooth shapes around each bone: chest, waist, calves, biceps, a closed fist on the handle, a tee, shorts and shoes. It is plain on purpose, so the muscles stand out.

    4. Muscles

      Each muscle runs from where it starts to where it attaches. Each frame it gets its length, and it swells as it shortens, keeping its volume. It glows by how hard it works: front, side and rear deltoid, and the upper trap.

    5. Load

      For free weights, the load on the joint is weight × gravity × the sideways distance to the dumbbell. Cables and machines will each get their own resistance curve.

    6. Automatic checks

      These run before an exercise ships:

      • smooth speed (the check that exists today);
      • joints stay in their normal range;
      • hands stay on the handle and feet stay planted;
      • muscle lengths stay between 55% and 120% of rest;
      • the mistake really differs on every fault it names.

    Costs and risks
    
        
      
          
    • Size: the 3D library (three.js, MIT licence) is 150 KB compressed (measured), loaded only when a guide opens. Each exercise adds about 1 KB.
      • 
          
    • Phone speed and battery: 3D uses the graphics chip. This needs a check on your phone. The fallback is the still pictures.
      • 
          
    • No 3D support: older phones get the still pictures and the same text.
      • 
          
    • Accuracy: muscle start and attachment points and the motions follow published anatomy and studies, but they are modelled, not motion-captured. Each new muscle needs its points checked.
      • 
          
    • Rework: the flat rig built today would be retired.

    3. Mirrored demos make you flip left and right in your head, and a 3D figure you can turn helps you read the pose. Virtual Reality, 2026
      4. 
          
    4. For learning body movements, animations can beat still pictures. Wong et al.
      5. 
          
    5. Above about 30°, the shoulder blade rotates about 1° for every 2° the arm rises (scapulohumeral rhythm). Physiopedia

    6. Raise slightly in front of the body, and don't turn the thumbs down: rotating the arm inward raises upper-trap and rear-delt work. EMG study, 2020
      7. 
          
    7. A dumbbell's load climbs to shoulder height and is lightest at the bottom. Dumbbell vs cable study
