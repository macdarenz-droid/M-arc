# Safe handling: grip, thumb, wrist, setup, and where you feel it

Research for the Technical Plate "hand zoom" and "feel it here" layers. Written 2026-09-30.
Scope: general rules that apply across exercises. Per-exercise detail comes later and should point back here.

## How to read the evidence tags

Every claim carries one tag and at least one source number (full list at the end).

- **[DATA]** measured in a study (EMG, imaging, dynamometer, injury records).
- **[MECH]** follows from basic mechanics or anatomy (lever arms, which bone carries load). No study needed to show a lever works, but nobody has measured it for this exact gym case.
- **[CONSENSUS]** what coaching and certifying bodies teach (NSCA, ACE, experienced coaches). Sensible and widely agreed, but not tested head to head.
- **[WEAK]** little or no direct evidence found. Say so in the app or leave it out.

## 1. Wrist position under load

### 1.1 What happens when you press with the wrist bent back

When you push a handle, the force comes back into your hand along the line of the push. Where that force lands in the hand decides what the wrist has to do.

- **Handle high in the fingers, wrist bent back.** The load sits in front of the wrist joint, out toward the fingers. That gap is a lever arm. Every kilo on the handle now tries to bend the wrist further back, and the muscles and ligaments on the palm side have to hold it, while the back of the wrist gets squeezed. Heavier load or a longer gap means more of this. [MECH] Coaches describe the same thing: the bar should sit low in the palm "to align properly with the forearm"; too much backward bend "strains the joint". [CONSENSUS] (S6)
- **What gets irritated.** People who hurt on the back of the wrist when loading it in a bent-back position (push-ups, planks, yoga) were imaged in one study: 84% had a visible problem on the back of the wrist, most often a small hidden ganglion cyst (76%), then partial tears of the scapholunate ligament. [DATA] (S4) This is a clinic sample of people who already had pain, so it tells us what is found in painful wrists, not how often lifting causes it.
- **The little-finger side (TFCC).** The ulna, on the little-finger side, carries about 20% of the load that goes through the wrist; the radius, on the thumb side, carries the rest. The triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC) sits at that ulnar side and spreads the load. [DATA] (S1, S2) TFCC injuries are common in sports that combine wrist twisting, sideways bending and axial load. [DATA, athlete sample] (S3) So a wrist that is bent back and also tipped toward the little finger under a heavy load is asking the smallest, most delicate part of the wrist to do more work. [MECH]
- **Extensor irritation.** Keeping a wrist braced against a bending load for many heavy reps also works the forearm tendons hard; the NHS lists wrist tendonitis and sprain among the usual causes of wrist pain after overuse. [CONSENSUS] (S31) I found no study that measured tendon load on a machine chest press specifically. [WEAK for the specific case] For scale: across the weight-training sports, the wrist and hand are among the most often injured sites, though overall injury rates are low. [DATA] (S17)

### 1.2 What "stacked" means

Stacked means the load passes in a straight line through the knuckles, the wrist, and the forearm, so the bones take it instead of the soft tissue.

- For a press: the handle sits in the heel of the palm, low in the hand, lined up over the forearm. The knuckles point up and sit over the wrist, which sits over the forearm. At the bottom of a barbell press the bar is over the wrist and the wrist is over the elbow. [CONSENSUS] (S6, S7)
- "Straight" does not mean zero degrees. A small backward tilt is normal and fine as long as the handle stays over the forearm; coaches allow "a slight backward bend ... allowing the bar to sit securely over the forearm". [CONSENSUS] (S6) ACE's wording for machines is "wrists in line with your forearms". [CONSENSUS] (S7, S8)
- Keep the wrist level side to side too: no tipping toward the little finger. The ulnar side is the weaker load path (see 1.1). [MECH from DATA] (S1, S2)

### 1.3 Where the handle should sit

| Movement | Where it sits | Wrist | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Presses (chest press, shoulder press, bench, dumbbell press) | Heel of the palm, low, over the forearm. Fingers close around it but do not carry it. | Stacked, small backward tilt at most | Force goes straight down the forearm bones, mostly the radius. [MECH, CONSENSUS] (S1, S2, S6) |
| Pulls (pulldown, rows, curls, pull-ups) | Across the fingers and upper palm, like carrying a bag | Level or slightly back. Never curled forward | Grip is strongest with the wrist a little extended (people chose about 35 degrees back; 15 degrees less than that cost a third of grip strength). A curled wrist weakens the grip, so the forearms tire before the back. [DATA] (S5) ACE asks for a neutral wrist on rows. [CONSENSUS] (S8) |

Note the difference: for pulls a little backward tilt helps the grip; for presses the tilt must stay small because the push comes through the hand. Both rules come from the same idea: keep the load on the bones and let the grip muscles work where they are strongest.

### 1.4 The owner's two photos in these terms

(Described from the task brief; I did not see the photos myself.)

- **Photo 1, the one that hurt.** The handle sat in the fingers, the wrist was bent far back, and the load passed behind the wrist line. On a heavy set this puts a large lever on the wrist every rep. The back of the wrist is squeezed and the palm-side tissue and forearm tendons hold the load. Soreness the next morning fits overload of those tissues. [MECH] (S4, S6) It is not a diagnosis; see section 6 for when to get it checked.
- **Photo 2, the right one.** The handle sits in the heel of the palm, the knuckles are stacked over the wrist, the wrist over the forearm. The push goes straight down the forearm bones. [MECH, CONSENSUS] (S6, S7)

Plate copy for the hand zoom (coach voice):

> Set the handle low in your palm, right on the heel of your hand. Your knuckles point up and stay over your wrist. If your wrist is folding back and the handle is sitting in your fingers, stop, reset the grip and try again with less weight.

> Your wrist should feel quiet on this. If you feel the back of your wrist squeezing, the handle has slid up into your fingers.

## 2. Thumb positions

### 2.1 The grips

| Grip | What it is | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Full, or closed | Thumb wraps around the handle, opposite the fingers | NSCA textbook definitions via study summaries (S14); Baechle and Earle drill: "Perform all grips with your thumbs around the bar" (S13) |
| Thumbless (open, false, "suicide") | Thumb sits on the same side as the fingers | (S14) |
| Hook | Thumb wraps the bar first, then the index and middle finger pin it | (S18) |
| Alternated (mixed) | One palm faces you, one faces away | (S14) |

### 2.2 When each is used

- **Full grip is the default for everything**, and it is required for any bar or dumbbell held over your face, neck or chest. The thumb is what stops a bar rolling out of the hand. [CONSENSUS] (S13, S14)
  - The safety case is real: weights falling on the lifter were the most common way people got hurt in US emergency-department records of weight-training injuries (65.5% of cases, 1990 to 2007). [DATA] (S16) A forensic report describes deaths from a barbell compressing the neck during bench press and at least 11 such deaths reported to the US product safety agency from 1999 to 2002. [DATA] (S15) Those records do not say which grip was used, so they support "keep the bar in your hands and use safeties", not a precise risk figure for the thumbless grip. [honest limit]
- **Thumbless on presses: don't.** On a barbell or dumbbell press a thumbless grip removes the one thing that stops the bar slipping. [CONSENSUS] (S13, S14, S15) On a plate-loaded or stack machine the handle cannot fall on you, so the danger is smaller; still teach the full grip, because the thumb also keeps the handle from drifting up into the fingers. [MECH]
- **Thumbless on pulls (pulldown, pull-up, rows): optional.** Many coaches suggest it so the fingers act as hooks and the forearms and biceps relax. I found no controlled study that measured thumb position on lat or biceps activity. [WEAK] Related data: changing grip type on the lat pulldown did not change biceps activity much. [DATA] (S20, S21) So in the app this is a comfort option, not a rule.
- **Hook grip:** Olympic lifts and heavy deadlifts, where the hands must hold a bar that is moving fast or very heavy. It hurts the thumb at first. [CONSENSUS] (S18) Not needed for the machine-based work this app mostly covers.
- **Alternated grip:** heavy deadlifts only. Not a default. [CONSENSUS] (S14)

### 2.3 Recommended default per movement family

| Family | Thumb |
|---|---|
| Any barbell or dumbbell press, anything over the face | Full grip, always |
| Machine presses (chest, shoulder) | Full grip, relaxed. Push with the heel of the hand, fingers loose |
| Pulls on handles or bar | Full grip by default; thumb-over allowed if the forearms give out first |
| Carries, holds, deadlifts | Full grip; hook only for trained lifters on heavy pulls |
| Handles used only for balance (leg press, hamstring curl) | Light full grip, no pulling |

Plate copy:

> Wrap your thumb around the bar. It is the thing that keeps the bar in your hand.

> On pulldowns you can put your thumb on top of the bar with your fingers if your forearms are burning out before your back. Keep your wrists straight either way.

## 4. Machine setup principles

1. **Line the working joint up with the machine's pivot.** ACE: "aligning the mid-point of your knee joint with the axis of rotation of the moving lever". The same idea applies to the elbow on a preacher or arm-curl machine and the knee on a leg extension. [CONSENSUS] (S11) Why: the machine is built to turn around one point; if your joint turns around a different point, the pad slides along the limb and the joint is pushed or pulled sideways. [MECH]
2. **Seat height puts the handles where the target muscle works.** Chest press: handles "level with your mid-chest (around nipple level)". Seated row: handles about level with the shoulders, chest pad touching lightly. [CONSENSUS] (S7, S8) Keep the elbows well below shoulder height on presses; a clinical guide for injured athletes recommends limiting how far the upper arm lifts out to the side and not letting the hands travel behind the chest. [CONSENSUS, clinical] (S12)
3. **Back, hips and head on the pad.** Free-weight bench work uses five-point contact (head, upper back, hips on the bench, both feet on the floor). [CONSENSUS] (S14) On machines, ACE asks for the back "firmly supported" and the shoulder blades staying on the backrest, and for the back and tailbone flat on the leg press. [CONSENSUS] (S7, S10)
4. **Range-of-motion stops.** Set the start so the handle is not behind your chest at the bottom of a press. Stop presses just before the elbows lock, and leg presses just before the knees lock. [CONSENSUS] (S7, S10, S12)
5. **Before the first rep.** Push the selector pin all the way in; a pin that is half in can let the stack drop. Never pull the pin while the stack is lifted. [CONSENSUS, manufacturer guidance] (S32) For barbell presses, set the safety bars or catches and use a full grip; the neck-compression deaths above happened on the bench press. [DATA] (S15, S16)

Plate copy:

> Set the seat so the handles line up with the middle of your chest. If they sit up near your collarbones, go down a notch.

> Line your knee up with the pivot on the side of the machine before you load it.

## 5. Where you feel it

### 5.1 What EMG can and cannot tell us

- Surface EMG measures the electrical signal over a muscle. A higher reading means more electrical activity there under those conditions. It does not directly measure force, it cannot tell you how much a muscle will grow, and comparing the signal between different muscles or setups needs care. A review in Frontiers in Physiology says conclusions drawn from EMG comparisons "are frequently unsubstantiated and unwarranted". [DATA, methods review] (S25)
- "Feeling it" is a sensation, not EMG. The burn and pump come from fatigue and blood flow in the working muscle. So the honest line is "this is where you should feel it working", not "this activates 80% of your chest".

### 5.2 Mind-muscle connection: what the studies show

- Telling trained lifters to focus on the chest raised chest EMG during bench press by about 22% at 50% of max, with front-shoulder and triceps activity unchanged; at 80% the chest cue raised chest and front-shoulder activity together. [DATA, n=11] (S26)
- Focus on a muscle raised its activity at 20 to 60% of max, but not at 80%. [DATA] (S27)
- A later study found that focus cues raised triceps activity but not chest activity on the bench. [DATA] (S28)
- Over 8 weeks, untrained men who focused on the muscle grew their biceps more (12.4% vs 6.9% thickness) but their quads the same. [DATA, small study, n=30] (S29)
- Focusing on the muscle can lower force output: an outward focus (move the handle) produced more torque than an inward focus in elbow flexion. [DATA] (S30)

What this means for copy: a "feel it here" line helps most on lighter, controlled sets (isolation work, warm-ups, 8 to 15 reps). On heavy sets, an outcome cue ("push the handles away") is at least as good. The evidence is mixed and the studies are small, so claim modestly. [honest summary of S26 to S30]

### 5.3 How to write an honest "feel it here" line

1. Name the muscle in plain words and the area on the body ("middle and lower chest", "the sides of your back under your armpits").
2. Name the most common wrong place and one fix that is actually about setup or technique.
3. Base the target on the exercise's accepted prime movers, and where there is EMG data on variants (bench angle, grip), follow it. Example: flat pressing works the middle and lower chest about as much as the front shoulder; steeper incline (45 degrees and up) shifts work to the front shoulder. [DATA] (S24)
4. No percentages, no "activates", no "engage", no hype.

Plate copy, owner's own example turned into the style:

> You should feel this across the middle and lower chest. If the front of your shoulders is taking over, lower the seat a notch and keep your elbows a bit lower.

### 5.4 Common "wrong place" signs and what they usually mean

| Exercise | Wrong place | What it usually means | Fix | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Machine chest press | Front of the shoulders | Handles set too high (seat too low), so the press turns into an incline press; shoulders rolling forward off the pad; elbows flared high | Handles at mid-chest; shoulder blades back on the pad; elbows a bit lower | Incline shifts work to front delts [DATA] (S24); setup [CONSENSUS] (S7, S12). The seat-height link is inferred from the incline data. [MECH] |
| Seated or bent-over row | Lower back | Leaning back and forth to swing the weight; a standing bent-over row loads the low spine the most of the rows tested | Chest on the pad or torso upright; lighter weight | Low-back load data [DATA] (S23); ACE cues [CONSENSUS] (S8) |
| Leg raise | Front of the hips, top of the thighs | That is normal to a point: leg raises are a hip-flexor exercise first. The abs work to hold the pelvis, and only in two-leg versions | Tilt the pelvis back at the top (tail toward you); bend the knees if the low back arches | Hip flexors highly active in leg lifts; abs needed in two-leg lifts only [DATA] (S22) |
| Lat pulldown | Biceps and forearms | Pulling with the hands; curled wrists; pulling the bar too low and behind | Hands as hooks, drive the elbows down to the sides; stop when the elbows stop going down | Grip width barely changes lat activity; pronated grip slightly more lat [DATA] (S20, S21); cue [CONSENSUS] (S9) |
| Any press | Wrist | Handle high in the fingers, wrist folding back | Handle to the heel of the palm; reduce load | [MECH, CONSENSUS] (S6); section 1 |
| Hamstring curl | Lower back | Hips lifting off the pad; too heavy | Hips down; lighter weight | "excessive recruitment ... of the lumbar extensors" with poor technique [CONSENSUS] (S11) |

Plate copy:

> On pulldowns you should feel it in the sides of your back, under your armpits. If your biceps are doing most of it, loosen your grip a bit and pull your elbows down to your sides.

> On a leg raise the front of your hips will work too, that's normal. If your lower back lifts off the pad, bend your knees and slow down.

## 6. When to stop and see someone

Short and calm. Based on NHS guidance for wrist pain (S31):

- Stop the set if you feel sharp pain, a pop, a snap or grinding.
- See a doctor or physio if:
  - the pain hasn't eased after about two weeks of rest and easy use,
  - it keeps coming back or gets worse,
  - you have tingling, numbness or pins and needles in the hand,
  - your grip feels weak or you struggle to hold things,
  - the wrist is swollen, warm and stiff.
- Get urgent help if the pain is severe, the wrist has changed shape or colour, you can't move it, or you've lost feeling in your hand.

Plate copy:

> A bit of soreness the next day is common. Sharp pain, tingling, a weak grip or swelling that doesn't settle within a couple of weeks is worth getting checked.

## 7. What the plate needs from this (hand-off, architecture only)

Per exercise, the later spec can reference these fields. Each value points back to a section above.

- `gripFamily`: G1 to G12 (section 3). Drives which hand zoom is drawn.
- `thumb`: full, full-relaxed, thumb-over-optional, hook (section 2.3).
- `contact`: heel-of-palm, fingers-and-palm, whole-foot, pad name (sections 1.3, 3).
- `wristRule`: stacked, level, stacked-small-tilt (section 1).
- `faultDrawing`: the wrong version for the zoom (for presses: handle in the fingers, wrist folded back, load line passing behind the wrist; the owner's photo 1).
- `feelHere`: muscle area for the body-map shimmer, plus one line of copy (section 5.3).
- `wrongPlace`: area, likely cause, one fix (section 5.4).
- `evidence`: tag and source IDs, so the app can show "coaching consensus" vs "measured" if we ever want to.

Risks and mitigations:
- Overclaiming "you'll feel X" when EMG is weak. Mitigation: plain area words only, tags kept in the data, no numbers in the copy.
- A wrist rule read as "zero degrees" makes people fight a natural small tilt. Mitigation: the copy says "stays over your wrist" and "handle in the heel of your hand", never "perfectly straight".
- Medical tone. Mitigation: section 6 stays short, NHS-based, and is only linked from the hand zoom, not shown on every exercise.
